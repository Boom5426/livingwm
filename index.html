About

Life is never static. Its images mostly are.

Living systems are never static: molecules switch conformations, cells respond to perturbations, tissues remodel, and diseases progress. Yet most visual data in biology and medicine are snapshots: fixed, sparse, and often destructive.

The Living World Models workshop asks how computer vision can learn world models of such systems: inferring biological states from images, forecasting how they evolve, simulating responses to perturbations and treatments, and generating faithful counterfactuals. Bringing together researchers in world models, generative modeling, 4D vision, microscopy, and medical imaging, we aim to define the methods, benchmarks, and evaluation standards for predictive vision of life.

What is a living world model?

Let x be an image of a biological system (a micrograph, a slide, a scan, a particle image), st its latent biological or clinical state at time t, and a a condition such as a perturbation, a drug, a treatment, or elapsed time.

  1. ximage
  2. Encoderqφ(s | x)infers the state
  3. ststate
  4. Transitionpθ(st+Δ | st, a)under condition a
  5. st+Δfuture state
  6. Decodergψ(x | s)renders an image
  7. x̂predicted

Because snapshots are usually unpaired, the transition is often identifiable only at the population level, as a map that carries the state distribution ρt to ρat+Δ. Optimal transport, flow matching, and Schrödinger bridges are natural tools for this setting.

Key questions

Same pipeline, two worlds

Biology and medicine ask what vision world models ask: how does the state of a system change under an intervention? But they ask it under conditions that break each assumption of current world models. Talks, papers, and the panel are organized around the four questions this creates.

One question, four scales

The same template connects communities that rarely meet at the same venue.

  • Molecules

    Observedcryo-EM particles, predicted structures

    Conditionligand, mutation

    Can the model generate a conformational ensemble consistent with heterogeneous particle images?

  • Cells

    ObservedCell Painting, live-cell and high-content imaging

    Conditioncompound, CRISPR edit, dose, time

    Can it predict morphology under unseen perturbations or their combinations?

  • Tissues

    Observedhistopathology, spatial omics, organoid imaging

    Conditiontreatment, development

    Can it infer unobserved molecular state from morphology and forecast remodeling?

  • Patients

    Observedlongitudinal MRI/CT, endoscopic and surgical video

    Conditiondisease course, therapy, surgical action

    Can it forecast the next scan or the outcome of an action with calibrated uncertainty?

Topics

Five themes

The first two themes anchor the broad biomedical-vision community. The last three define what is distinctive about this workshop.

  1. Visual representations of biological and clinical states

    Representation learning for microscopy, Cell Painting, histopathology, and medical imaging. Alignment of images with molecular, spatial-omics, and clinical signals.

  2. Prediction and generation of state changes

    Image-based prediction of perturbation responses, image generation under drug and genetic interventions, disease-progression and longitudinal-imaging forecasting, counterfactual image generation, virtual staining and cross-modal generation.

  3. Learning dynamics from sparse and destructive observations

    Optimal transport, flow matching, and Schrödinger bridges between distributions. Dynamics from unpaired populations, sparse longitudinal data, and cryo-EM heterogeneity. Predicting motion or change from a single image.

  4. Structure, motion, and simulation across scales

    From static structures to conformational ensembles. Organ shape and motion. Surgical and endoscopic scene simulation. 3D and 4D reconstruction of biological structures.

  5. Evaluation and trustworthiness of visual simulators

    Scientific metrics and benchmarks for generative simulators, out-of-distribution generalization, uncertainty estimation, hallucination detection, and interpretability.

General-purpose methods for world models, video generation, and 4D vision are in scope whenever the submission argues for their relevance to a question in the life sciences.

Review process

Both tracks use double-blind review on OpenReview, and every author needs a complete OpenReview profile. At least one reviewer per paper has domain expertise in the relevant biological or clinical setting. The criteria are relevance, technical soundness, quality of evidence, and value for discussion. Organizers and reviewers recuse themselves from conflicted submissions, including those from their own institutions, and an organizer without a conflict oversees each decision.

Responsible submissions
  • Papers that use clinical data state the data source, the de-identification, and the ethics approval or data-use agreement. Public datasets are used under their licenses.
  • Synthetic cell images and medical scans are clearly labeled as synthetic.
  • Accepted papers state their intended use and limitations. Predictive models of disease progression are not validated clinical tools.
  • We encourage reporting of subgroup performance and dataset composition.
  • The workshop follows the CVPR 2027 policy on large language models for authors and reviewers. Reviewers may not upload submissions to external AI services.
Awards and student support

We will present a Best Paper award and a Best Student Paper award. The non-archival track welcomes biology-first and medicine-first submissions that use vision methods, and an online mentoring hour, held by the organizers before the camera-ready deadline, supports first-time CVPR authors.

When is a generated cell or scan scientific evidence?

The panel question

Speakers from vision, biology, and medicine debate how to evaluate generative simulators, when predictions are trustworthy enough to guide experiments or care, and which benchmarks the community should build next. Audience questions, including questions submitted online, take the last 15 minutes.

Taking part remotely

One organizer relays online questions in every Q&A slot. Authors who cannot travel may present through a prerecorded video, with their poster displayed by an organizer or coauthor. An asynchronous discussion channel stays open for two weeks after the workshop. Accepted papers, posters, and slides (with author consent) will be posted on this website, and the organizers will publish a short summary of open problems and proposed evaluation practices within four weeks of the workshop.
