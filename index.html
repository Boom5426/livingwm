About

Life is never static. Its images mostly are.

Living systems are never static: molecules switch conformations, cells respond to perturbations, tissues remodel, and diseases progress. World models, which learn the state of an environment, predict how it evolves under actions, and generate its future, offer a natural framework for such systems.

The Living World Models workshop asks how to learn world states of living systems from biomedical visual observations, predict how these states evolve over time and under interventions, and generate trustworthy future states. Biology makes each step hard. Images reveal only part of the state, observations are often destructive snapshots rather than trajectories, most interventions are never tested, and generated outputs must hold up as scientific evidence. Bringing together researchers in world models, generative modeling, 4D vision, microscopy, and medical imaging, we aim to define the methods, benchmarks, and evaluation standards for world models of living systems.

What is a living world model?

Let x be an image of a biological system (a micrograph, a slide, a scan, a particle image), st its latent biological or clinical state at time t, and a an intervention such as a perturbation, a drug, a treatment, or elapsed time. A living world model has three components.

  1. ximage
  2. Encoderqφ(s | x)learns the world state
  3. ststate
  4. Transitionpθ(st+Δ | st, a)predicts dynamics under a
  5. st+Δfuture state
  6. Decodergψ(x | s)generates the future
  7. xt+Δfuture image

Because snapshots are usually unpaired, the transition is often identifiable only at the population level, as a map that carries the state distribution ρt to ρat+Δ. Optimal transport, flow matching, and Schrödinger bridges are natural tools for this setting.

Key questions

Three components, two worlds

The workshop is organized around one core question. How can we learn world states of living systems from biomedical visual observations, predict how these states evolve over time and under interventions, and generate trustworthy future states? Vision world models answer it for video, where the state is mostly visible and realism is the test. Living systems raise one key question at each component, and talks, papers, and the panel are organized around these three.

Topics

Four themes

The first three themes follow the components of a living world model. The fourth is the evaluation that makes its outputs trustworthy.

  1. World states from biomedical observations

    Representation learning for microscopy, Cell Painting, histopathology, and medical imaging. Latent states under partial observability. Alignment of images with molecular, spatial-omics, and clinical signals. 3D and 4D reconstruction of biological structures.

  2. Dynamics under interventions

    Image-based prediction of responses to chemical and genetic perturbations. Disease-progression and longitudinal-imaging forecasting. Optimal transport, flow matching, and Schrödinger bridges between distributions. Dynamics from unpaired populations, sparse longitudinal data, and cryo-EM heterogeneity. Generalization to unseen interventions and their combinations.

  3. Generation of future states

    Image and video generation conditioned on drugs, genes, treatments, and time. Counterfactual image generation. Virtual staining and cross-modal generation. Conformational ensembles beyond static structures. Organ motion and surgical and endoscopic scene simulation.

  4. Evaluation and trustworthiness

    Scientific metrics and benchmarks for generative simulators, out-of-distribution generalization, uncertainty estimation, hallucination detection, and interpretability.

General-purpose methods for world models, video generation, and 4D vision are in scope whenever the submission argues for their relevance to a question in the life sciences.

Review process

Both tracks use double-blind review on OpenReview, and every author needs a complete OpenReview profile. At least one reviewer per paper has domain expertise in the relevant biological or clinical setting. The criteria are relevance, technical soundness, quality of evidence, and value for discussion. Organizers and reviewers recuse themselves from conflicted submissions, including those from their own institutions, and an organizer without a conflict oversees each decision.

Responsible submissions
  • Papers that use clinical data state the data source, the de-identification, and the ethics approval or data-use agreement. Public datasets are used under their licenses.
  • Synthetic cell images and medical scans are clearly labeled as synthetic.
  • Accepted papers state their intended use and limitations. Predictive models of disease progression are not validated clinical tools.
  • We encourage reporting of subgroup performance and dataset composition.
  • The workshop follows the CVPR 2027 policy on large language models for authors and reviewers. Reviewers may not upload submissions to external AI services.
Awards and student support

We will present a Best Paper award and a Best Student Paper award. The non-archival track welcomes biology-first and medicine-first submissions that use vision methods, and an online mentoring hour, held by the organizers before the camera-ready deadline, supports first-time CVPR authors.

When is a generated cell or scan scientific evidence?

The panel question

Speakers from vision, biology, and medicine debate Key Question 3: how to evaluate generated future states, when predictions are trustworthy enough to guide experiments or care, and which benchmarks the community should build next. Audience questions, including questions submitted online, take the last 15 minutes.

Taking part remotely

One organizer relays online questions in every Q&A slot. Authors who cannot travel may present through a prerecorded video, with their poster displayed by an organizer or coauthor. An asynchronous discussion channel stays open for two weeks after the workshop. Accepted papers, posters, and slides (with author consent) will be posted on this website, and the organizers will publish a short summary of open problems and proposed evaluation practices within four weeks of the workshop.
